# Soft Skills — Communicating, Collaborating, and Working Responsibly

## Audience and prerequisites

For students entering professional work and early-career individual contributors, including technical professionals. Prerequisites: everyday reading, writing, and experience participating in group activities. No programming, management experience, particular accent, or outgoing personality is required.

Scope: professional responsibility, self-awareness, listening, spoken and written communication, teamwork, feedback, priorities, collaborative judgment, conflict, negotiation, and professional relationships. Treat soft skills as behaviours that depend on context and can be practised, rather than fixed personality traits. Introduce cultural differences, accessibility, unequal authority, confidentiality, and ethical boundaries where they first affect a decision.

Target capability: explain communication and coordination problems, compare possible responses, and justify responsible choices using evidence, context, and the needs of other people. Worked demonstrations support understanding; completion does not establish assessed interpersonal competence or guarantee career outcomes.

Structure: **11 chapters, 53 sections**, distributed **4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, and 5**. Counts follow conceptual scope and do not imply equal teaching duration.

## Learning spine

Understand responsibilities and personal reactions → listen and establish shared meaning → express ideas clearly → communicate across channels → coordinate work → exchange useful feedback → manage commitments → reason together → address conflict → negotiate and influence → integrate habits and continue development.

Why this progression: listening supplies the basis for audience-aware expression. Spoken and written communication support everyday coordination; specific feedback becomes easier to understand after roles and expectations are explicit. Capacity and decision reasoning prepare learners for disagreement over competing interests. Negotiation combines those earlier capabilities. Real situations involve several skills at once; the sequence introduces their prerequisites rather than prescribing a universal interaction script.

Recurring example: a cross-functional team preparing an internal reporting launch. An unclear request becomes a shared goal, dependencies create competing deadlines, a late defect prompts a difficult release decision, and the team reviews what happened. Technical details are explained only as needed. A service handover and a newcomer asking for help provide contrasting contexts.

Teaching method: objective → explanation and mental model → annotated dialogue or workplace artefact → contrasting response and consequences → learning outcome → bridge. Use message rewrites, meeting records, responsibility maps, capacity sketches, and decision tables. State assumptions and distinguish plausible consequences from guaranteed reactions. Optional follow-along is welcome; submitted exercises, assessments, and learner monitoring are not required. Keep this document at course-plan level.

## Chapter 1 — Professional Responsibility and Self-Awareness

Objective: Establish what responsible participation looks like and distinguish observable conduct from assumptions about personality or intent.

1. Workplace expectations, shared goals, and the meaning of professionalism
2. Observable behaviour, interpretations, and assumptions about other people
3. Recognising personal reactions and choosing a considered response
4. Reliability, accountability, confidentiality, and ethical boundaries

Worked illustrations: Compare two responses to a missed launch commitment: hiding the delay and acknowledging its impact with a revised plan. Separate facts, interpretations, emotions, and proposed actions in a short incident account. Contrast taking responsibility with accepting blame for circumstances outside one's control. Explain why confidentiality limits what can be shared even when openness would be convenient.

Learning outcome: Explain how behaviour affects shared work and select a response that combines accountability with realistic boundaries.

Bridge: Responsible action requires understanding what other people actually need and mean.

## Chapter 2 — Listening, Questions, and Shared Understanding

Objective: Explain how listening and inquiry reduce avoidable misunderstanding without assuming that understanding means agreement.

1. Attention, interruption, and listening for meaning rather than preparing a reply
2. Open, focused, and clarifying questions and when each helps
3. Paraphrasing, summarising, and checking interpretation
4. Perspective-taking, empathy, and distinguishing intent from impact
5. Language differences, cultural context, and accessible participation

Worked illustrations: Trace a request for a report to be ready “soon” through questions about audience, required content, and deadline. Annotate a paraphrase that invites correction and a leading question that suppresses useful information. Compare silence caused by agreement, uncertainty, processing time, and lack of opportunity; show why asking is preferable to decoding intent from appearance. Offer spoken and written routes for clarification.

Learning outcome: Compare listening and questioning choices and explain how to confirm meaning while leaving room for disagreement and correction.

Bridge: Once the need is understood, a speaker must organise an answer the audience can use.

## Chapter 3 — Clear Spoken Communication

Objective: Organise explanations around an audience's purpose and communicate evidence, uncertainty, and requests clearly.

1. Audience, purpose, context, and the desired next step
2. Main point, supporting reasons, evidence, and a clear request
3. Explaining unfamiliar concepts, limitations, and uncertainty
4. Pace, pauses, audibility, and supportive visual or written information
5. Presentations, questions, and acknowledging what is not yet known

Worked illustrations: Explain the same reporting defect to an analyst and a project sponsor, changing detail while preserving facts. Compare a chronological technical monologue with a decision-focused update. Annotate a brief presentation with its main point, evidence, limitation, and request. Contrast an invented answer with a clear acknowledgement and a plan to verify it. Discuss delivery in terms of intelligibility and access rather than accent conformity, compulsory eye contact, or supposed universal body-language signals.

Learning outcome: Explain how audience and purpose shape a spoken message and justify ways to make it clear, credible, and accessible.

Bridge: Messages also need to remain understandable when the author is absent and readers respond later.

## Chapter 4 — Written and Asynchronous Communication

Objective: Design workplace messages that make meaning, responsibility, and next steps easy to find.

1. Selecting a channel for urgency, complexity, sensitivity, and traceability
2. Clear requests and updates using audience-aware language and structure
3. Tone, ambiguity, evidence, and revising before sending
4. Decisions, handovers, shared records, and appropriate information access

Worked illustrations: Rewrite an unclear team message into a request with context, owner, deadline, and confirmation of the next step. Compare a short status update with a decision record; explain why they serve different purposes. Show how a sensitive disagreement can move to a conversation followed by an agreed written summary. Contrast concise writing with omission of essential caveats. Avoid placing confidential details in an unnecessarily broad channel.

Learning outcome: Compare channels and interpret how message structure, tone, and records affect action and misunderstanding.

Bridge: Clear individual messages become more useful when a team agrees how work is coordinated.

## Chapter 5 — Teamwork and Coordination

Objective: Explain how shared goals, explicit responsibilities, and inclusive coordination support dependable team work.

1. Shared goals, roles, responsibilities, and decision authority
2. Dependencies, handovers, and confirming completion
3. Meetings with a purpose, participation, decisions, and follow-up
4. Trust, inclusion, and making it possible to raise concerns
5. Accountability, repairing coordination failures, and changing team needs

Worked illustrations: Map the reporting launch's dependencies and identify a task that everyone assumed someone else owned. Compare a meeting transcript with no conclusion against a record of decisions, owners, and unresolved questions. Explain how inviting concerns differs from acting on them and protecting participation. Use a newcomer joining the team to show why working agreements may need revision; team-development models are useful lenses rather than inevitable stages.

Learning outcome: Explain coordination failures and justify explicit agreements that support participation, ownership, and reliable handovers.

Bridge: Teams need a way to discuss the behaviours that help or obstruct those agreements.

## Chapter 6 — Giving, Receiving, and Using Feedback

Objective: Distinguish useful feedback from personal judgment and explain how feedback can lead to a specific, revisitable change.

1. Feedback purpose, timing, privacy, and relationship context
2. Describing a situation, observable behaviour, and its impact
3. Asking about intent and exploring different accounts
4. Receiving, clarifying, and evaluating feedback without automatic agreement
5. Agreeing changes, offering support, and checking what happened next

Worked illustrations: Replace “you are careless” with a specific description of a missed handover and its effect. Annotate a Situation–Behavior–Impact conversation followed by inquiry into intent. Compare accurate but poorly timed feedback with a private, purposeful conversation. Show a recipient correcting a factual error, accepting a useful point, and asking for an example. Contrast an agreed behavioural change with a vague promise to improve.

Learning outcome: Explain the components and limits of constructive feedback and compare responses that preserve inquiry and accountability.

Bridge: Some repeated failures arise from competing demands and capacity rather than a lack of care.

## Chapter 7 — Priorities, Commitments, and Adaptability

Objective: Make trade-offs visible and explain how to revise commitments responsibly when circumstances change.

1. Clarifying importance, urgency, consequences, and who sets priorities
2. Available capacity, effort uncertainty, dependencies, and interruptions
3. Making realistic commitments and negotiating what can fit
4. Communicating delays, asking for help, and maintaining boundaries
5. Adapting plans and learning from setbacks without treating overload as personal failure

Worked illustrations: Sketch a team's available capacity against two requested deadlines, making estimates and uncertainty explicit. Compare silently accepting incompatible requests with presenting options to the relevant decision owner. Trace the effect of a late dependency on the launch plan. Show a delay update that states impact, choices, and required help. Introduce a simple prioritisation table as an aid to discussion, not a universal productivity formula or a promise to eliminate stress.

Learning outcome: Explain competing commitments and justify a realistic revision using capacity, dependencies, consequences, and appropriate authority.

Bridge: Choosing between revised plans requires a shared way to frame problems and compare options.

## Chapter 8 — Collaborative Problem-Solving and Judgment

Objective: Structure a shared decision using a clear problem, explicit assumptions, meaningful options, and a plan to revisit the result.

1. Defining the problem, desired result, constraints, and decision owner
2. Distinguishing evidence, inference, missing information, and uncertainty
3. Generating options before comparing their consequences
4. Challenging assumptions, seeking dissent, and avoiding premature agreement
5. Recording a decision, checking results, and revisiting assumptions

Worked illustrations: Compare releasing the report with a documented limitation, postponing it, and narrowing its scope. Construct a decision table using stated criteria and explain who supplied them. Show how a missing fact changes the preferred option, and why numerical scores cannot remove judgment. Contrast blaming a colleague with investigating the failed handover. Distinguish a poor outcome caused by uncertainty from a decision made without considering available evidence.

Learning outcome: Explain a collaborative decision and justify option comparisons while acknowledging assumptions, uncertainty, and revision conditions.

Bridge: A well-structured problem does not eliminate disagreement about consequences, relationships, or values.

## Chapter 9 — Conflict and Difficult Conversations

Objective: Explore disagreement constructively while recognising boundaries and situations requiring support or escalation.

1. Task disagreement, relationship tension, and differences in power
2. Preparing facts, concerns, desired outcomes, and a suitable setting
3. Opening a difficult conversation and exploring competing perspectives
4. Responding to emotion, repairing misunderstandings, and setting boundaries
5. Pausing, seeking support, escalating, and following organisational procedures

Worked illustrations: Annotate a conversation about repeated handover failures, distinguishing observation from accusation and a request from a threat. Compare a disputed technical choice with a pattern of intimidating conduct; explain why the same conversational approach may be inappropriate. Show how to pause an unproductive exchange and record an agreed next step. Do not imply that empathy or a polished script obliges someone to tolerate harmful behaviour or guarantees a safe response from a more powerful person.

Learning outcome: Explain conflict dynamics and compare conversation, pause, support, and escalation options for the circumstances.

Bridge: When discussion reveals incompatible requests, the next task is to negotiate acceptable commitments.

## Chapter 10 — Negotiation and Influence Without Authority

Objective: Explain how to explore interests, alternatives, and criteria while preserving honest communication and acceptable limits.

1. Positions, underlying interests, constraints, and stakeholder concerns
2. Alternatives to agreement, limits, and when declining is appropriate
3. Generating options and discussing fair, relevant criteria
4. Ethical persuasion through evidence, consequences, and listening
5. Explicit agreements, responsibilities, contingencies, and follow-through

Worked illustrations: Negotiate reporting-launch scope between a sponsor seeking speed and a team concerned about reliability. Separate a fixed launch-date position from the underlying business need. Compare options using agreed criteria and describe what happens if no acceptable agreement is reached. Contrast persuasive evidence with concealing known risks. Record the resulting scope, owners, exceptions, and conditions for reconsideration; agreement is not inherently better than an unacceptable commitment.

Learning outcome: Explain a negotiation and justify influence choices using interests, alternatives, evidence, boundaries, and explicit agreements.

Bridge: These capabilities become more dependable through professional relationships and repeated reflection on real work.

## Chapter 11 — Professional Relationships and Continued Development

Objective: Integrate the course's ideas and explain how to continue developing them through purposeful relationships and feedback.

1. Asking for help, offering support, and respecting others' time
2. Mentoring, purposeful networking, reciprocity, and boundaries
3. Explaining contributions and learning in career conversations
4. Integrating communication, collaboration, judgment, and reliability
5. Reflective practice, useful feedback, and selecting a next behaviour to develop

Worked illustrations: Trace the reporting launch from initial request to retrospective, showing where listening, written records, commitments, feedback, and negotiation interact. Compare a vague help request with one that states the problem, attempted approach, and specific question. Annotate a short account of a professional contribution without exaggerating individual ownership. Transfer the ideas to a newcomer navigating a service handover. Model a reflection that identifies one observable change and an appropriate source of feedback rather than claiming a transformed personality.

Learning outcome: Explain how the course's capabilities reinforce each other and outline a realistic path for continued development in a specific workplace context.

Bridge: Continue practising in ordinary conversations, messages, meetings, and commitments; seek feedback on specific behaviours, compare intentions with effects, and revise approaches as relationships and responsibilities change.

Evidence limits: the plan identifies teaching topics and worked cases; it is not a systematic evidence review of interventions. Detailed efficacy claims about attention, prioritisation, decision biases, delivery, and inclusive practice require further primary research before lesson authoring. No universal communication percentages, lie-detection rules, personality tests, or fixed productivity prescriptions are included.

Boundaries: inclusion and ethical judgment recur throughout; specialist diversity training, employment law, formal mediation, clinical care, sales tactics, and people-management systems require separate depth. Career conversations are introductory transfer examples; CV writing and interview preparation are separate topics. Technical problem-solving is illustrated only to explain collaboration, with specialist engineering methods left to their subject courses.
